Appendix A — Some Mathematical Support

This appendix collects short explanations of ideas that support the SVM material.

A.1 Convex Hulls

In mathematics, a hull is some form of smallest set containing a collection of points. In isolation, clearly this is very vague, but we usually consider a type of hull such as a convex hull.

Consider a collection of points drawn on a page and imagine stretching a rubber band around the outside of those points. Once you let go of the rubber band, the shape enclosed by the rubber band, the remaining shape is the convex hull of the points.

The convex hull includes:

  • the original points
  • the straight-line edges between the outermost points
  • every point inside the enclosed region

The key word is convex. A region is convex if the straight line between any two points in the region also stays inside the region.

A.1.1 Formal Definition

For points \(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\), the convex hull is the set of all convex combinations:

\[ \left\{\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i x_i : \lambda_i \geq 0,\ \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i = 1 \right\}. \]

The coefficients \(\lambda_i\) are non-negative and add to one. This means a convex combination is a weighted average of the original points.

A.2 Primal and Dual Problems

In mathematics, and more specifically optimisation, the concepts of dual and primal relate to the perspective in which a problem is viewed. At its core, the primal problem is the direct problem, the natural perspective of the problem; whilst, the dual problem is a less natural perspective which may offer an easier solution. Both the primal and dual have the same solution; it just comes down to mathematical ease associated with finding it.

Let us consider the maximal margin classifier problem as an example. The primal problem asks:

Which boundary should we choose?

The dual problem asks a different but equivalent question:

Which training points should be allowed to influence the boundary, and by how much?

We should already know that the optimal boundary is completely defined by a few observations known as support vectors. The dual problem directly connects the optimisation to these support vectors. To see this, we can take a deeper look at the optimisation.

A.2.1 The Primal Problem

For the hard-margin classifier, we established the natural, primal, optimisation by

\[ \min_{b,\mathbf{w}} \frac{1}{2}\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2 \]

subject to

\[ y_i(\mathbf{w}\cdot \mathbf{x}_i + b) \geq 1, \qquad i = 1,\dots,n. \]

Recall the interpretation of this:

  • choose the boundary by finding \(\mathbf{w}\) and \(b\)
  • make the margin around the boundary as wide as possible
  • make sure to correctly classify every observation

This is the direct interpretation from the geometric intuition used to explain SVMs. We know that this optimisation problem should be well behaved, it is a quadratic objective with linear constraints; however, that does not necessarily mean that it is easy to solve.

A.2.2 A Dual Perspective

By introducing \(y_i\) as an encoder, the primal easily characterises one margin constraint for each observation. The dual introduces one coefficient, \(\alpha_i\), for each of these constraints. Here, \(\alpha_i\) measures the importance of the \(i^{th}\) observation on the boundary.

Therefore, two cases exist:

  • if \(\alpha_i = 0\), the observation has no direct influence on the boundary
  • if \(\alpha_i > 0\), the observation is actively determining the boundary

These “active” observations are the support vectors. The dual problem can then be phrased as an optimisation of these \(\alpha_i\) coefficients, essentially optimising which observations should be important for defining the optimal boundary. This is achieved with a little bit of optimisation theory from Lagrange, and a little more elbow grease (by that, we obviously mean taking some derivatives).

A while later, the dual optimisation problem is given by

\[ \max_{\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n} \left[ \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i - \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j x_i^\top x_j \right] \]

subject to

\[ \alpha_i \geq 0, \qquad i = 1,\dots,n, \]

and

\[ \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i y_i = 0. \]

Notice that the dual relies on the

  • the class labels \(y_i\)
  • the coefficients \(\alpha_i\)
  • dot products between pairs of observations, \(\mathbf{x}_i \cdot \mathbf{x}_j\)

More importantly, we no longer directly optimise over \(\mathbf{w}\) or \(b\). But this is fine,

After solving for the set of \(\alpha_i\), the boundary \(\mathbf{w}\) is obtained via

\[ \mathbf{w} = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i. \]

Therefore, the optimal boundary is built as a linear combination of observations. Furthermore, since most observations are associated with an \(\alpha_i = 0\), they do not contribute to the sum. The remaining observations, with \(\alpha_i > 0\), are the support vectors.

A.2.3 Support Vectors Actively Constrain The Boundary

We have said that the support vectors actively constrain the optimisation. This is related to \(\alpha_i\) because the dual solution satisfies the equation

\[ \alpha_i\left[y_i(\mathbf{w}\cdot \mathbf{x}_i + b) - 1\right] = 0. \]

This product can be zero in two ways:

  • \(\alpha_i = 0\), so the point does not influence the solution
  • \(y_i(\mathbf{w}\cdot \mathbf{x}_i + b) = 1\), so the point lies exactly on the margin

Therefore, if \(\alpha_i > 0\), the point must lie on the margin.
